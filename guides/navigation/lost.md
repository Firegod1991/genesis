# Lost: find your way with a compass, the sun and the stars

A pocket guide for the day the phone is dead and the trail is gone. Read it now, while nothing is wrong. Practice every part of it once on a walk you already know, because the middle of being lost is the worst time to learn.

The full manuals are on the same shelf: Basic Land Navigation from the National Wildfire Coordinating Group and the navigation chapter of the US Army survival manual.

## Rule one: STOP

The first thing a lost person does is walk faster. That is how a small problem becomes a search.

* **Stop.** Sit down. Drink some water. You are not in danger from standing still for ten minutes.
* **Think.** Where did you last know exactly where you were? How long ago, and roughly which way have you walked since?
* **Observe.** Hills, ridges, water, roads, power lines, the sound of traffic, the sun. What can you see from here?
* **Plan.** Decide one thing to do and do it. Then stop and think again.

**Stay put if anyone knows where you went.** A person who stays in one place is found far sooner than one who keeps moving. Children should be taught to hug a tree and wait.

## Know your compass

A baseplate compass is the kind to own. Its parts:

* **Direction of travel arrow.** Printed on the clear plate. It points where you are going.
* **Housing.** The ring you turn, marked 0 to 360 degrees. The mark at the top of the housing, in line with the travel arrow, is the **index line**. You read your bearing there.
* **Orienting arrow.** The outline of an arrow printed inside the housing. It turns with the housing.
* **Needle.** Its red end points to magnetic north. It does not point to the North Pole.

**Keep metal away.** A phone, a knife, a belt buckle, a car or a power line will pull the needle. Hold the compass flat at your waist, away from all of them.

### The one move to learn: red in the shed

Almost everything you do with a compass ends the same way: turn something until the red end of the needle sits inside the orienting arrow. Some say "put red Fred in the shed."

### Take a bearing to something you can see

1. Point the direction of travel arrow at the landmark: a peak, a lone tree, a tower.
2. Turn the housing until red is in the shed.
3. Read the number at the index line. That is your bearing to the landmark. Write it down.

### Walk a bearing

1. Turn the housing until the bearing you want sits at the index line.
2. Hold the compass flat in front of you and turn your whole body until red is in the shed.
3. The travel arrow now points the way. Look along it and pick something you can see on that line: a rock, a tree.
4. Walk to it without looking at the compass. Then do it again.

Walking with your eyes on the needle drifts you off line. Walking landmark to landmark does not.

### The way back

The **back bearing** takes you home along the line you came. Add 180 to your bearing if it is under 180, or take 180 away if it is 180 or more. A bearing of 60 has a back bearing of 240. A bearing of 300 has a back bearing of 120.

## Map and compass together

### Declination: the map and the compass disagree

The lines on a map point to true north. The needle points to magnetic north. The angle between them is the **declination**, printed in the margin of most maps, and it changes from place to place and slowly over the years. In parts of the western United States it is more than 10 degrees, enough to miss a trailhead by a long way after a few miles.

* **East declination:** going from the map to the compass, take the declination away. Going from the compass to the map, add it.
* **West declination:** the other way round. Map to compass, add it. Compass to map, take it away.

The saying for map to compass: **east is least, west is best.** A compass with an adjustable declination lets you set it once and forget the sums.

### Turn the map to match the land

Set the housing to 0 (plus or minus the declination as above). Lay the compass on the map with its edge along a north line. Turn map and compass together until red is in the shed. Now the map lies the way the land does: the hill on your left is the hill on the left of the map.

### Take a bearing off the map

1. Lay the edge of the compass along a line from where you are to where you want to go, travel arrow toward the goal.
2. Turn the housing until its north lines run along the map's north lines, orienting arrow toward the top of the map.
3. Read the index line. That is the true bearing. Correct it for declination to get the bearing to walk.

### Find where you are: two landmarks

When you can see two things you can also find on the map, you can find yourself.

1. Take a bearing to the first landmark. Correct it to a true bearing. Work out the back bearing.
2. Draw a line on the map from that landmark along the back bearing.
3. Do the same for the second landmark, ideally one that sits off to the side, near a right angle from the first.
4. You are where the two lines cross. A third landmark makes a small triangle. You are inside it.

## Know how far you went

**Count your paces.** Count each time your left foot lands. On flat ground most adults take 60 to 70 of these to cover 100 meters. Find your own number once on a measured stretch, such as a running track, and keep it written in your kit. Hills, sand, snow, brush and a heavy pack all add paces.

Keep the count with knots in a cord or beads you slide: one for every 100 meters.

## Tricks that save you

* **Handrails.** Follow something long that you cannot lose, a river, a ridge, a road, a fence line, even when it is not straight toward the goal.
* **Catching features.** Know what lies just past your goal, a stream or a road. If you reach it, you went too far.
* **Aim off.** Heading for a bridge on a river? Aim deliberately to one side of it, say to the left. When you hit the river you know the bridge is to your right. Aiming straight at it, you would not know which way to turn.
* **Go around, come back to the line.** Blocked by a cliff or a swamp? Count paces going sideways, walk past it on your bearing, then walk the same number of paces back. You are on your line again.

## No compass: the sun

### The shadow stick

1. Push a straight stick upright into flat ground. Put a stone at the tip of its shadow.
2. Wait 15 to 20 minutes. Put a second stone at the new tip.
3. A line from the first stone to the second runs west to east. The first stone is west.
4. Stand with the first stone on your left and the second on your right. You are facing north. This works anywhere on Earth.

It is most accurate close to midday and on level ground.

### The sun across the day

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west, though only on two days of the year exactly so. In summer it rises and sets toward the north, in winter toward the south. When it is highest in the sky, around midday, it lies due south for anyone north of the tropics, and due north for anyone south of them.

### An analog watch

* **North of the tropics:** hold the watch flat and point the hour hand at the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and the 12 is south. When the clocks are on summer time, use the 1 instead of the 12.
* **South of the tropics:** point the 12 at the sun. Halfway between the 12 and the hour hand is north.

It is rough, worst near midday and near the equator. A phone that still shows the time is enough: draw the watch face on the ground.

## No compass: the night sky

### North: the North Star

1. Find the **Big Dipper**, seven bright stars shaped like a ladle.
2. The two stars at the end of the bowl, away from the handle, are the **pointers**.
3. Follow the line from the bottom pointer through the top one, about five times the gap between them. The first fairly bright star on that line is **Polaris**, the North Star, at the end of the handle of the Little Dipper.
4. Polaris sits within one degree of true north. Drop a line straight down from it to the horizon: that is north.

When the Big Dipper is low or hidden, find **Cassiopeia**, a W of five stars on the other side of Polaris. Polaris lies about the same distance from Cassiopeia as from the Dipper.

How high Polaris stands above the horizon, in degrees, is close to your latitude. A fist held at arm's length covers about 10 degrees.

### South: the Southern Cross

South of the equator Polaris is out of sight. Find the **Southern Cross**, four bright stars in a kite, next to two very bright **pointer stars**. Run a line along the long arm of the cross, about four and a half times its length. Drop straight down from that empty point to the horizon: that is south. A line drawn halfway between the pointers, at right angles to them, crosses the same point.

### Orion

The middle of Orion's belt rises very close to due east and sets very close to due west, wherever you are.

### Any star, with two sticks

Push in two sticks, one short and one taller, so that sighting over their tops you see a star. Wait about 15 minutes and look again from the same place.

* The star has **risen**: you are facing east.
* It has **sunk**: you are facing west.
* North of the tropics, it has moved **right**: you are facing south. **Left**: north.
* South of the tropics, left and right swap.

## Signs that lie

**Moss does not grow only on the north side of trees.** It grows where it is wet and shaded, which can be any side. Do not steer by it, or by which side of a tree has more branches.

**Following a stream downhill** often leads to roads and people, but it can also lead over waterfalls, into cliffs and into thick brush that is slow and dangerous. Follow it from the high ground beside it, not down the channel.

## Getting found

* **Three of anything means help.** Three blasts on a whistle, three fires in a triangle, three flashes. Rest, then repeat. A whistle carries much farther than a voice and costs no strength.
* **A mirror reaches miles.** Hold out your other hand in a V, catch the sun on the hand, then sweep the flash across the V onto the aircraft or the far hillside.
* **Write on the ground.** Big letters, at least three meters long, in a color that stands out: logs, stones, cut branches, a tarp. **SOS** is understood everywhere. To an aircraft, **V** means you need help and **X** means you need a doctor.
* **Be seen.** Spread anything bright in the open. Stay near it. Make smoke in daylight and light at night.

## Before you go: what to carry

* A baseplate compass, and a map of the area on paper. Know the declination before you leave.
* A whistle on a cord around your neck.
* A light with spare batteries, and a small mirror.
* Water, a layer more than you think you need, and something to eat.
* Tell someone where you are going and when you will be back.

The Trail Sense app in the phone apps shelf works with no signal at all: a compass, a backtrack of your route, sunrise and sunset, and a clinometer. It is only good while the battery lasts. The compass in your pocket is good forever.
