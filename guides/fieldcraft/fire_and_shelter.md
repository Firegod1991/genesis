# Fire and shelter: stay warm through the night

Cold kills more people lost outdoors than hunger ever does. In bad weather a person can be in danger within hours. Shelter first, then fire, then water, then food.

The US Army survival manual on this shelf has the full chapters, with drawings.

## Shelter

### Pick the spot

* **Out of the wind** and above where water runs or pools. Never in a dry streambed: a storm miles away can send a flood down it.
* **Not under dead trees** or dead branches. They fall in wind, and they fall at night.
* **Not on a bare ridge,** where the wind is worst, and **not at the very bottom of a valley,** where cold air pools at night. Partway up a slope is warmest.
* Close to dry firewood and water, but not on top of the water.

### Insulate from the ground first

The cold ground pulls heat out of a body faster than cold air does. Before anything else, build a bed: a thick pile of dry leaves, pine needles, grass or branches, as deep as your forearm is long and deeper still in snow. Squeezed flat, it should still be a hand thick.

### Keep it small

A shelter only as big as your body warms up. A big one never does.

* **Tarp:** tie a line between two trees at waist height, hang the tarp over it, and stake the edges down low to the side the wind comes from.
* **Debris hut:** lean a long pole from the ground onto a stump or forked stick at hip height, lean sticks along both sides to make a ribbed tent just big enough to lie in, then pile leaves and debris over it, an arm's length thick. Crawl in feet first and plug the door with a pack or more leaves.
* **A car** is a shelter. Stay with it: it is easier to find than you are.
* **Snow:** dig in on the side away from the wind and keep a hole open for air.

### Stay dry and covered

* Wet clothes lose most of their warmth. Cotton is the worst. Wool and synthetics keep some warmth when wet.
* Take off layers before you sweat while working, and put them back on before you cool down.
* Cover your head and neck. Keep socks dry. A dry pair for sleeping is worth its weight.

## Fire

### What a fire needs

Heat, fuel and air. Take away any one and it dies. Most fires fail for lack of air or for wet, too thick fuel.

### Gather everything first

Collect it all before you strike anything, and collect twice what you think you need.

1. **Tinder:** catches a spark. Dry grass, the papery bark of birch, the inner bark of cedar rubbed into fluff, pitch wood (fatwood) from old pine stumps, a bird's nest, dryer lint, cotton balls with petroleum jelly from your kit.
2. **Kindling:** dead twigs from pencil lead to pencil thick. Snap them: dry ones crack, wet ones bend.
3. **Fuel:** sticks as thick as a thumb, then a wrist, then a forearm.

Take dead wood that is standing or hanging, not lying on wet ground. Split a wet stick and the inside is often dry.

### Build and light it

1. Clear the ground down to dirt, a stride all round. Keep it away from overhanging branches, tents and dry grass.
2. Put a platform of dry sticks under the fire if the ground is wet or snowy.
3. A fist sized nest of tinder. Lean kindling over it in a cone, smallest sticks in, with a gap on the side the wind comes from.
4. Light the tinder low, on the side the wind comes from. Shield it with your body.
5. Feed it slowly, small to large. Blow gently at the base. Do not smother it with big wood too soon.

### Ways to light it

* **Lighter or matches:** keep them in a sealed bag. Waterproof matches and a second lighter in the kit.
* **Ferro rod:** hold the striker still against the tinder and pull the rod back, hard and fast. Works when wet.
* **Batteries:** touch steel wool to both ends of a 9 volt battery, or bridge a gum wrapper's foil between the ends of an AA. It flares at once: have tinder ready.
* **Sun:** a magnifying glass, reading glasses or the bottom of a clear bottle focused to the smallest bright point on dark tinder.

### Never inside

**Never burn charcoal, use a camp stove, run a generator or keep a fire inside a tent, a car, a garage or a closed house.** Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills people in their sleep. Generators belong outside, at least 20 feet from doors and windows.

### Put it out

Drown it, stir it, drown it again, until you can hold your hand flat on the ashes. Dirt alone can leave coals that burn for days.

## Signal while you wait

A fire in daylight makes a column of smoke when you throw green leaves or grass on it. Three fires in a triangle are an international call for help. Keep the signal fire built and ready, and light it when you hear an aircraft.
