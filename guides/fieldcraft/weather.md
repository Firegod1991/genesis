# Reading the weather with no forecast

With no phone and no radio, the sky, the wind and the air still say what the next day holds. None of this is certain, and all of it works best together: one sign is a hint, three are a forecast.

## Clouds

* **Small puffy clouds with flat bottoms** on a sunny morning: fair weather. Watch them through the day.
* **Puffy clouds growing taller by early afternoon,** towering like cauliflower: thunderstorms are likely by late afternoon.
* **A dark, towering cloud with a flat, spreading top like an anvil:** a thunderstorm. Lightning, hail, strong wind, sudden heavy rain.
* **High, thin wisps like horses' tails,** then a thin white veil that slowly thickens, lowers and covers the sky: rain or snow is likely within a day or two. A **ring around the sun or the moon** in that veil says the same.
* **Low gray layer, flat and even:** drizzle, or a dull day.
* **Clouds moving fast in different directions at different heights:** the weather is changing.

## Wind

* **A steady breeze that suddenly shifts or dies, with a cool gust from the direction of dark clouds:** a storm is minutes away.
* In much of North America weather comes from the **west.** A wind that swings round from the east and south often brings rain; one that swings to the west and north after rain often brings clearing and colder air.
* **Calm, clear night with heavy dew or frost by morning:** the next day is usually fair. A cloudy night stays warmer, with no dew.

## Pressure

A barometer is the best weather tool you can carry. Many phones have one: an app that reads altitude or pressure works with no signal.

* **Falling fast** over three hours: a storm is coming.
* **Falling slowly:** the weather is turning worse.
* **Rising:** clearing.
* Standing still while your phone's altitude reading **climbs** means the pressure is falling.

## Old sayings that hold up, and one that does not

* **"Red sky at night, sailor's delight. Red sky in the morning, sailor's warning."** Often right where weather comes from the west: a red sunset means clear air to the west, a red sunrise means clear air leaving to the east and clouds coming.
* **Smoke that sinks or hangs low** in the morning, and smells that carry strongly, often come before rain.
* **"Heat lightning" does not exist.** It is lightning from a storm too far away to hear. That storm may be coming.

## Lightning

**When thunder roars, go indoors.** If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Wait **30 minutes** after the last thunder before going back out.

Caught outside with nowhere to go:

* Get off peaks, ridges and open ground. Go down.
* Stay away from lone trees, poles, fences, water and anything metal.
* A car or a closed building is safe. A tent, a picnic shelter or a carport is not.
* In a group, spread out at least 50 feet apart, so one strike does not hit everyone.
* Keep moving toward a safer place. Crouching on the ground does not make you safe.

Someone struck by lightning does not hold a charge: it is safe to touch them. Start CPR if they are not breathing.

## Flash floods

**Turn around, don't drown.** Six inches of moving water can knock you down. A foot of it moves most cars, and two feet can carry off a truck. Most flood deaths are people in vehicles driving into water.

* Never drive or walk into flowing water, or into water you cannot see the bottom of.
* Rain in the hills can flood a dry canyon or wash where you stand, under a clear sky. Camp high.
* If water rises around your car and it stalls, get out and climb to higher ground.

## Heat and cold

* **Heat:** hardest work early and late. Shade at midday. Drink before you are thirsty. Watch each other for confusion: see the first aid card.
* **Wind chill:** wind on wet skin pulls heat out fast. A day that feels mild can bring on hypothermia when you are wet and the wind rises. Get out of the wind and get dry.
